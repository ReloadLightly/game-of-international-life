# From IR theory to computational mechanisms

The central discipline of this project is to prevent a theory's name from becoming a decorative label. Every theory implementation should specify a complete chain:

> theoretical assumptions → actor information → decision rule → interaction process → macro observable → rival explanation → failure condition

A theory module should not be merged until this chain is explicit.

## Shared modeling vocabulary

The project distinguishes three levels:

1. **Cell:** a stable geographic or strategic site on a lattice.
2. **Polity/state:** an actor controlling one cell in M1 or a connected set of cells in M2 and later territorial models.
3. **System:** the distribution of capabilities, relations, positions, and outcomes generated by all actors.

M1 deliberately makes one cell one polity to isolate a security-dilemma mechanism. M2 separates cell identity from polity identity: control can change while the location remains the same, and multi-cell states aggregate production and capability.

## Shared M2 territorial mechanics

Later territorial theories should inherit the following rather than silently rebuilding their own worlds:

- odd-row six-neighbor hex geography;
- connected initial territories grown from reproducible seeds;
- local resource productivity;
- polity treasuries and capability aggregation;
- frontier dilution and local force concentration;
- common battle resolution, costs, fortification, and territorial transfer;
- extinction and connected-component succession;
- common measurements and matched random shocks.

The supplied M2 opportunistic target selector is **not** part of this immutable substrate. It is the first replaceable policy rule.

## Jervis: offense–defense and spiral–deterrence

### Theoretical ingredients

- anarchy and self-help;
- uncertainty or ambiguity about others' postures;
- offense–defense balance;
- ability to distinguish offensive from defensive postures;
- reciprocal security responses.

### M1 representation

| Concept | M1 representation |
|---|---|
| Polity | One square-lattice cell |
| Capability | Discrete arms level `0..max_arms` |
| Offensive/defensive posture | Fixed Boolean cell attribute |
| Strategic neighborhood | Eight-cell Moore neighborhood |
| Distinguishability | Weight suppressing threat attributed to defensive neighbors |
| Offense advantage | Relative multiplier on attack and defense effectiveness |
| Spiral | Growth in mean arms from reciprocal local threat responses |
| Conflict | Local threshold event when attack capacity exceeds neighborhood deterrence |

### First discriminating interventions

- change distinguishability while holding the exact initial world constant;
- change offense advantage while holding all actor attributes constant;
- introduce signal noise without changing actual postures;
- introduce a single offensive polity into otherwise defensive worlds;
- compare synchronous and asynchronous updates.

### Current simplifications

- offensive posture is exogenous and fixed;
- posture type stands in for intention, doctrine, weapon technology, and signaling;
- all polities use the same response rule;
- the Moore neighborhood is a stylized strategic topology;
- there is no bargaining, learning, territory, or alliance layer in M1.

These are assumptions to relax one at a time, not defects to conceal.

## Waltz: structural realism

### Theoretical ingredients

- anarchy as the system's ordering principle;
- functionally similar units;
- variation primarily in capabilities;
- survival and self-help;
- systemic selection, competition, and socialization;
- internal and external balancing;
- polarity as a property of capability distribution.

### Translation onto M2

The Waltzian design should minimize heterogeneity in actor objectives and decision rules. States differ chiefly in capability and position. M2 already supplies:

- endogenous territorial size and resource production;
- capability aggregation;
- effective number of powers and concentration measures;
- state survival and extinction;
- geography, borders, and projection constraints.

M3 must add a balancing rule whose inputs and outputs are explicit. Candidate inputs include:

- own capability share;
- strongest rival capability and growth;
- local and system-wide concentration;
- defensible frontier and resource exposure;
- expected internal-balancing return;
- later, availability and reliability of external partners.

### Candidate macro observables

- effective number of powers;
- capability concentration and polarity persistence;
- internal-balancing expenditure;
- survival distribution;
- war frequency and territorial turnover;
- convergence of initially different actor behaviors under selection pressure.

### Failure condition

A “Waltzian” outcome is not informative if bipolarity or balancing is directly programmed as the actor's target. Structure should constrain and select behavior; the expected system pattern must not be copied into the local objective.

## Mearsheimer: offensive realism

### Theoretical ingredients

- anarchy;
- offensive military capability;
- uncertainty about intentions;
- survival as the primary goal;
- strategic rationality;
- incentives to maximize relative power and seek regional hegemony.

### Translation onto M2

M2 already provides the material opportunities and constraints required for territorial power competition:

- resources from controlled cells;
- offensive projection into adjacent territory;
- defense, fortification, and frontier dilution;
- successful conquest increasing future production;
- endogenous territorial concentration and state extinction.

M4 should replace the generic opportunity rule with a relative-power policy that evaluates not only immediate battle advantage but the change in own and rival capability shares. Regional hegemony must remain emergent rather than assigned.

### Matched rival model

The meaningful experiment compares offensive realism with a security-seeking rule in the **same** M2 worlds. Both receive identical geography, resources, information, battle mechanics, and shocks. They differ in whether expansion remains valuable after a defensible security threshold has been reached.

### Candidate observables

- attacks undertaken after security sufficiency;
- preventive and opportunistic war;
- territorial and capability concentration;
- emergence and persistence of regional hegemons;
- balancing responses against rising powers;
- survival of power maximizers versus security seekers;
- systemic resource and war costs.

## Snyder: alliance politics

A fixed-neighborhood lattice is insufficient because alliance ties need not coincide with geography. The appropriate form is a **networked spatial model**: M2 territory plus a dynamic graph.

### Local alliance variables

- partner set;
- commitment strength;
- dependence on each partner;
- perceived reliability;
- adversary alignment;
- cost of fighting for an ally;
- cost of being abandoned;
- risk of entrapment.

### Decisions

- tighten or loosen commitment;
- support, withhold support, or defect during a crisis;
- seek a new partner;
- balance or bandwagon;
- pass responsibility to another ally.

### Candidate observables

- alliance-bloc formation;
- abandonment and entrapment rates;
- chain-ganging cascades;
- buck-passing;
- alliance durability;
- conflict diffusion;
- interaction between territorial polarity and alliance dilemmas.

### Crucial trade-off

Increasing commitment should reduce abandonment risk while increasing the probability or cost of entrapment. A rule that makes stronger alliances costless is not a Snyderian model.

## Cross-theory experiments

The most informative experiments hold the world fixed and vary only policy rules:

- security-seeking sufficiency versus relative-power maximization;
- Waltzian balancing versus bilateral opportunity seeking;
- Jervisian reassurance-sensitive actors versus worst-case responders;
- Snyderian flexible alliances versus unconditional commitments;
- homogeneous rule populations versus mixed theoretical ecologies;
- hand-coded theories versus evolved interpretable rules.

A theory can fail in several precise ways:

- it does not generate its expected pattern;
- it produces the pattern only in a tiny parameter region;
- a rival rule produces the same pattern more robustly;
- it matches final outcomes through the wrong process;
- its predicted direction reverses under small topology or information changes;
- it collapses in held-out maps or shocks;
- its supposed signature is observationally equivalent to a simpler mechanism.
