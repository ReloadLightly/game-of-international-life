# Research program

## Core question

> Which forms of international order and disorder can be generated by explicit local strategic rules under anarchy, and which rival mechanisms produce distinguishable artificial histories?

Game of International Life is a cumulative computational research program rather than a collection of unrelated simulations. Each milestone adds one necessary layer while preserving earlier worlds as transparent baselines.

The long-term aim is to connect four traditions:

1. canonical cellular automata and artificial life;
2. spatial and agent-based models of world politics;
3. substantive international-relations theory;
4. evolutionary discovery of interpretable strategic rules.

## Why cellular automata?

Cellular automata impose theoretical austerity. They require the modeler to specify:

- what exists at a site;
- what information is locally available;
- when actions occur;
- whether updates are synchronous;
- how interaction changes the next state;
- which macro-patterns are genuinely emergent.

They are especially useful for borders, neighborhood threats, diffusion, cascades, path dependence, territorial expansion, state formation, and critical transitions. They give systemic IR theory a literal computational form: no central governor is needed, yet system-level structure can constrain and emerge from unit interaction.

A lattice is not universally appropriate. Long-distance alliances, institutions, markets, and communication should be implemented as graph or global layers rather than forced into geographic adjacency.

## Scientific ladder

### Level A — Formal and software correctness

- verify neighborhood and boundary semantics;
- preserve synchronous updates where claimed;
- reproduce canonical Conway patterns;
- test geographic and political identity separately;
- guarantee deterministic replay for fixed seeds;
- enforce territorial contiguity and other state invariants;
- record executable configurations.

### Level B — Mechanism verification

- construct extreme cases with clear directional expectations;
- ensure theoretical controls are causally live;
- inspect process traces, not only final images;
- search for implementation shortcuts and accidental asymmetries;
- identify the conditions under which a mechanism becomes inactive.

M1's offense–defense controls and M3's post-sufficiency attack count are examples of direct mechanism checks.

### Level C — Discriminating theory experiments

- implement at least two rival rule families;
- place them in the same world;
- match initial conditions and exogenous shocks;
- declare hypotheses and failure criteria before interpreting results;
- report paired differences across ensembles;
- detect observational equivalence and divergent causal paths.

M3 is the first Level C experiment.

### Level D — Computational fossil-record replication

- reconstruct published spatial IR models as faithfully as surviving documentation permits;
- separate replication from modernization;
- reproduce qualitative and quantitative headline behavior;
- document ambiguities rather than silently inventing details;
- compare original rules with modern alternatives only after a faithful baseline exists.

Priority ancestors include Bremer and Mihalka's *Politics among Hexagons* and Cederman's models of emergent polarity and state formation.

### Level E — Evolutionary discovery

- evolve compact, inspectable local rules or small programs;
- use hand-coded theories as baselines and ancestors;
- evaluate across distributions of worlds rather than one map;
- hold out topologies, resource fields, and shock sequences;
- preserve behavioral diversity, not only scalar fitness;
- translate evolved rules back into theoretical propositions.

Candidate representations include:

- Boolean expressions over named local features;
- decision lists;
- finite-state transition tables;
- small typed genetic-programming trees;
- linear threshold rules;
- modular programs assembled from theory-bearing primitives.

### Level F — Empirical contact

- initialize geography, capabilities, alliances, and conflict exposure from documented data;
- estimate only parameters with defensible empirical meanings;
- separate calibration and evaluation periods;
- compare process traces where evidence permits;
- report abstraction error and uncertainty explicitly;
- avoid treating visual resemblance as validation.

## Cumulative milestone architecture

### M0 — Canonical Life

Purpose: establish correct CA semantics and a shared vocabulary of emergence.

Implemented:

- Conway B3/S23;
- fixed and toroidal boundaries;
- canonical still-life, oscillator, and glider tests.

### M1 — Jervisian security dilemma

Purpose: translate a recognizable IR mechanism into local state transitions.

Implemented:

- offense–defense advantage;
- distinguishability of offensive and defensive postures;
- local threat perception;
- reciprocal arming and conflict;
- matched four-world comparison and parameter surface.

Interpretive level: generative mechanism verification.

### M2 — Territorial state substrate

Purpose: create the common geopolitical world required for matched theory comparisons.

Implemented:

- six-neighbor hexagonal geography;
- stable cell identity and mutable political control;
- connected multi-cell states;
- spatial resources and polity treasuries;
- capability aggregation and frontier dispersion;
- attack, defense, conquest, extinction, and fragmentation;
- traceable successor states;
- ensemble trajectories and polarity measures.

Interpretive level: world-substrate verification.

### M3 — Structural-policy comparison

Purpose: isolate a core disagreement between security seeking and relative-power maximization.

Implemented:

- a replaceable policy protocol;
- shared theory-neutral attack candidates;
- security sufficiency and abstention;
- continued relative-power opportunity seeking;
- balanced and dominant-power starting structures;
- diffuse and clustered resource geographies;
- hashed matched worlds;
- encounter-keyed common random numbers;
- process and system outcome observables;
- paired and aggregate outputs.

Interpretive level: discriminating computational experiment.

### M4 — Alliance politics

Purpose: model Snyder's alliance security dilemma without reducing alliances to geography.

Minimum graph-layer state:

- partner set;
- commitment strength;
- dependence;
- perceived reliability;
- adversary alignment;
- assistance cost;
- abandonment exposure;
- entrapment exposure.

Minimum decisions:

- tighten or loosen commitment;
- support or withhold support during a crisis;
- seek or leave an alliance;
- balance, bandwagon, or pass responsibility.

First experiment:

```text
commitment flexibility × polarity × threat concentration
```

Primary process observables:

- abandonment;
- entrapment;
- chain-ganging;
- buck-passing;
- alliance turnover;
- conflict diffusion.

A stronger alliance must not be costless. The model is not Snyderian unless lowering abandonment exposure can raise entrapment risk.

### M5 — Perception, signaling, and territorial security dilemmas

Purpose: move Jervisian uncertainty onto the M2/M3 world.

Candidate additions:

- latent posture distinct from observed posture;
- noisy capability estimates;
- reassurance and costly signals;
- mobilization visibility;
- accidental escalation;
- deterrence failure.

### M6 — Evolutionary rule discovery

Purpose: search beyond the hand-coded theory set without losing interpretability.

Candidate research questions:

- Which compact policy survives the widest distribution of structural worlds?
- Which rules minimize expenditure subject to a survival constraint?
- Can evolution discover deterrence without persistent spirals?
- Can alliance rules navigate abandonment and entrapment better than hand-coded baselines?
- Do evolved policies recombine recognizable theoretical mechanisms?
- Which behavioral stepping stones precede novel stable orders?

The output of interest is not merely a winner. It is a rule whose behavior remains legible across counterfactual worlds.

### M7 — Fossil-record replications

Purpose: recover and modernize the intellectual lineage of computational IR.

Each replication should have two branches:

1. **faithful reconstruction**, preserving original assumptions as closely as possible;
2. **2026 extension**, introducing modern software, richer experiments, and evolutionary search without rewriting history.

### M8 — Empirical worlds

Purpose: establish carefully delimited contact with historical data.

Potential applications include regional security complexes, alliance crises, territorial rivalry, and changes in polarity. Empirical modules should be added only when the model's state variables and interventions can be mapped to evidence without hand-waving.

## Minimal experiment contract

Every substantive experiment should provide:

1. a precise research question;
2. a theory-to-mechanism translation;
3. policy-visible information;
4. shared and treatment-specific assumptions;
5. directional hypotheses;
6. failure criteria;
7. deterministic or seeded run command;
8. complete machine-readable configuration;
9. run-level and aggregate data;
10. figures generated from saved histories or tables;
11. sensitivity boundaries;
12. an interpretation separating software verification, mechanism evidence, and empirical claims.

## Evolutionary objectives worth studying

Scalar peace or raw territorial power are poor universal objectives. More interesting search targets include:

- robust survival under unknown structures;
- low systemic expenditure subject to minimum security;
- deterrence without uncontrolled conflict cascades;
- resilient cooperation under partner loss;
- alliance policies balancing abandonment and entrapment;
- policies that preserve optionality under partial observability;
- rules reproducing several stylized facts at once;
- novelty search for previously unseen but stable forms of international order.

Any evolutionary run should include hard spending or compute limits, hand-coded baselines, held-out worlds, and an archive of behaviors. Evaluation is supporting methodology, not the project’s substantive center.

## What success looks like

The project succeeds when it can produce one or more of the following:

- a faithful computational reconstruction of a major IR model;
- a clean counterexample to a claimed mechanism;
- a map of the structural conditions under which rival theories diverge;
- evidence that similar outcomes arise through different processes;
- an interpretable evolved rule that suggests a novel theoretical conjecture;
- a reusable artificial world in which IR scholars and complex-systems researchers can perform matched counterfactual experiments.

The standard is not visual spectacle. It is transparent causal structure plus reproducible artificial history.
